\begin{abstract}
KernelAscent asks whether a model can make itself better at writing GPU kernels, and whether that
improvement compounds. We built a five-rung ladder --- one-shot capability, weight-level
self-training, frozen-weight procedure self-editing, a closed model rewriting an open trainee's
harness, and self-play in which a model authors the frontier it must then beat --- and ran it
across scales from 0.5B to 32B.

Our primary finding is about the measurement rather than the models. In a controlled comparison
where two cells differ in one environment variable and nothing else, the benchmark's original
scoring rule reports a contrast of $-0.0002$ across ten rounds while the identical generations,
rescored, report $+0.436$. Best-of-$k$ scoring cannot distinguish a model that solves a task once
in $k$ from one that solves it $k$ times in $k$, which is precisely the quantity self-training
moves; and because a matched control arm reaches the saturation point within three or four
rounds, the blindness is scheduled by the design rather than risked by it.

The ladder's upper rungs did not return nulls. They returned undefined quantities, for one shared
reason that is computable in advance: the self-referential loop received almost no input. The
weight rung's learner received under one verified example per round with over 40\% of rounds empty; the
procedure rung's archive grew by four entries across six rounds, or none; the self-play author
had 4 of 173 proposals accepted. We therefore make no claim that recursive self-improvement fails
here, and we argue instead that a self-improvement benchmark must demonstrate its loop has
throughput before any contrast it reports carries information.

We report what survives with its scope attached, and Section~\ref{sec:removed} lists every claim
this project has retracted, narrowed, or excluded, each with the evidence that forced the change.
\end{abstract}
