A benchmark that reports a null owes its reader a second result: evidence that the
measurement \emph{could have} reported a positive. Self-improvement benchmarks are unusually
exposed here, because the headline quantity is a difference between two arms of the same
pipeline. Any defect that suppresses both arms equally leaves the contrast at zero while every
surface diagnostic --- jobs complete, kernels compile, scores populate --- looks healthy. A
broken instrument does not report that it is broken; it reports $\Delta \approx 0$.

Over this project, \textbf{seven independent pipeline defects each produced a plausible,
publishable-looking null}, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:defects}. None announced itself, and
three survived a code review before being caught by an assertion. Six were found only after
they had been written up as findings; one was caught before it reached prose, and we report
what made the difference.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.30\linewidth}p{0.31\linewidth}p{0.31\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Defect} & \textbf{Presented as} & \textbf{Caught by} \\
\midrule
Grader mis-imported under a wrong root & ``self-training does not compound'' & identity-kernel assertion \\
Grading device index absent on a 1--2 GPU allocation & ``the model writes bad kernels'' & same assertion, run \emph{on the allocation shape the jobs use} \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\paragraph{A clean zero is a bug hypothesis before it is a finding.}
The operational signature that caught most of these is distributional, not statistical. A
\emph{real} null is noisy: it has per-task spread, partial successes, seed-to-seed variance. A
null in which every task fails identically, or every score takes the same value, is
instrumentation. We therefore treat suspicious tidiness as evidence \emph{against} the result,
inverting the usual intuition that clean data is good data.

\paragraph{Correctness of the instrument is not sufficient; it must also have range.}
This is the failure mode we consider most transferable, because it defeats the obvious check.
Our headroom-normalised score is
$0.5 + 0.5\cdot\mathrm{clamp}\!\left(\frac{sp-1}{\mathrm{ceiling}-1}\right)$,
which is well-formed, was verified end-to-end on known-good inputs, and was working exactly as
specified. It was nonetheless useless as a contrast: on H100, \textbf{75\% of the frozen base's \emph{non-zero} scores on the 29-task bank land within $\pm0.01$ of parity} (18 of 24 solved tasks) --- precisely the value of a correct submission at
parity with the baseline. With both arms pinned to the same absorbing value,
$\textsf{lineage}-\textsf{reset}$ is zero \emph{by construction}, and the experiment could not
have detected compounding in either direction.

\paragraph{The sixth ``defect'' was our own explanation --- and so was its correction.}
This entry has been wrong twice, in opposite directions, and we report the sequence because the
second error is more instructive than the first.

\textbf{First claim.} We attributed the $0.50$ spike to \texttt{torch.compile} being relatively
stronger on H100, closing headroom that existed on A100.

\textbf{First correction, which we published.} We then found that the scorer takes the compiled
baseline only under \texttt{KA\_SCORE=compiled}, and that its \texttt{ceiling} argument defaults
to $1.5$ when unsupplied --- and concluded that the runs had used the eager baseline with a
fixed anchor, so that ``no claim about \texttt{torch.compile} was licensed by those runs''.

\textbf{That correction was false.} The cluster tooling exports
\texttt{KA\_SCORE=compiled} into \emph{every} job through its project environment, and has since
before any of these runs. The submitted batch scripts contain the line. Those runs therefore
\emph{did} score against the \texttt{torch.compile} baseline with the per-task roofline ceiling
--- exactly as pre-registered. We had checked the library default and never checked the
environment the jobs actually ran in.

\textbf{What the evidence now constrains.} A score of exactly $0.50$ requires
$sp_{\textrm{compiled}} = 1.0$ \emph{exactly} --- the submission matching the compiled reference.
That is consistent with two very different worlds: one where \texttt{torch.compile} is strong and
the model matched it, and one where \texttt{torch.compile} gives no speedup on these
memory-bound elementwise operators, so the compiled baseline \emph{is} the eager baseline and a
plain-torch rewrite lands at $1.0\times$ by construction. These are distinguishable without any
model: measure $t_{\textrm{eager}}/t_{\textrm{compiled}}$ per task. That probe is running, and
we report its result rather than choosing between the two stories now.

\textbf{The transferable lesson is about where a configuration lives.} We reasoned from the
library's default argument and the code path, which is where a programmer looks, and the answer
was in the job's environment, which is where the program actually ran. \textbf{A default is not
a setting.} An experiment's configuration is the union of its code, its invocation and its
environment, and only the last of those is invisible from inside the repository. This is the
same failure as the seventh defect below, where the host's \texttt{CC} leaked into a container
--- both are the environment silently overriding what the code appears to say.

\paragraph{Settling it by measurement: the compiled baseline is a real baseline.}
Rather than choose between the two stories above, we measured the quantity that separates them
--- $t_{\textrm{eager}}/t_{\textrm{compiled}}$ per task, with no model involved:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{\texttt{torch.compile} gain over eager} & \textbf{tasks (of 29)} \\
\midrule
$<1.1\times$ (no meaningful gain) & \textbf{0} \\
$1.1$--$1.5\times$ & 15 \\
$>3\times$ & 14 \\
\midrule
median $1.34\times$, max $21.85\times$ & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent \texttt{torch.compile} beats eager on \emph{every} task in the bank. So the compiled
baseline is not a naive reference, and the second story is the right one: a submission scoring
$0.50$ under the compiled scorer has \textbf{matched an optimised baseline}, which is a
meaningfully stronger statement than we had been making. The instrument was measuring something
harder than we credited it with.

This also resolves the numbers that looked contradictory. Under the \emph{calibration} scorer
(eager, fixed $1.5\times$ anchor, which \texttt{difficulty\_filter} invokes directly and which no
environment variable affects), a plain-torch rewrite lands at exactly parity --- hence the
cluster at $0.50$. Under the \emph{run} scorer (compiled, per-task roofline), the same rewrite
scores \emph{below} parity, which is why the frozen-base capability on those runs is $0.401$ and
not $0.50$. Two scorers, two distributions, one of which we had been quoting as the other.

\paragraph{The seventh defect: the benchmark had never measured its own subject.}
Having traced the plateau to the prompt, we tested the obvious follow-up --- remove the steer
and ask explicitly for a Triton kernel. The model solved \textbf{0 of 29} tasks, against 25 of
29 under the published prompt. That is a clean, pre-registered, publishable result: the models
try when asked and cannot produce a working kernel.

It is also false. Before reporting it we applied the known-good-input gate to the \emph{new}
input: we hand-wrote a Triton kernel that is correct by construction and required the real
grader to verify it, alongside a plain-torch control that must pass and a wrong kernel that
must fail. The controls behaved; the correct Triton kernel returned
\texttt{FileNotFoundError}. The cause was that Triton compiles a small C shim at first use and
takes its compiler from \texttt{\$CC}, which the cluster's module system sets to a
\emph{host} toolchain path that does not exist inside our container. Plain PyTorch never
compiles anything at runtime, so \textbf{only Triton broke} --- and Triton is the subject of the
benchmark. One environment variable fixed it, after which the same kernel verifies.

The consequence is not a corrected number but a corrected scope: for the entire history of this
project, \textbf{every submission that was not plain PyTorch failed for an environment reason}.
The benchmark has been measuring correctness-preserving rewriting, not kernel optimisation.

This also revises our own account of the previous defect. We attributed the saturated score to
the prompt's instruction to prefer plain torch --- but that instruction now reads as a
\emph{rational adaptation to a broken grader}. Tuned against a harness in which Triton always
failed, plain torch genuinely was the only thing that worked. The prompt and the grader were
coupled, and the grader came first. Generalising: \textbf{a configuration that looks
inexplicably conservative may be compensating for a defect}, and because the defect never
appears in any result, the workaround looks like a design choice indefinitely.

Two things made this one catchable before publication rather than after. First, failure reasons
now propagate: the grader had always computed a reason string and discarded it one line later,
so every failure in project history was a bare \texttt{False} --- and ``model writes Triton,
Triton grades \texttt{False}'' reads naturally as a statement about the model. Second, and more
transferable: \textbf{when an experiment begins producing a new \emph{kind} of artifact, the
known-good gate must be re-instantiated for that kind.} ``The grader works'' was true and
useless; ``the grader works on Triton'' was the question, and nothing was asking it.

\paragraph{What the corrected instrument then showed.}
With Triton verifying, the prompt A/B could finally be run as intended. Classifying every
generation (rather than counting only successes) across four tasks at $k{=}4$, $n{=}16$ per arm:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llrl@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Prompt} & \textbf{Outcome} & \textbf{$n$/16} & \textbf{95\% CI} \\
\midrule
published & plain-torch rewrite & 16 & [81\%, 100\%] \\
published & attempted a custom kernel & \textbf{0} & [0\%, 19\%] \\
\midrule
kernel & attempted a custom kernel & \textbf{14} & [64\%, 97\%] \\
kernel & \quad of which \emph{verified correct} & \textbf{1} & --- \\
kernel & \quad of which failed verification & 13 & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent This separates two quantities the project had conflated throughout. \textbf{The
prompt controls whether the model attempts a kernel at all} --- 0 attempts versus 14 is an
instruction being followed, not a capability difference. \textbf{Capability controls whether
the attempt succeeds} --- 1 of 14 verified. The model can write a working Triton kernel and
usually does not. With $n{=}16$ the interval is wide and the point estimate is not the claim;
the claim is that the rate is \emph{neither zero nor high}.

That distinction is the most consequential measurement in this report, because it explains every
saturation we chased. The 0.50 floor, the ceiling at 1.000, and a coverage-injection control
that exhausts its task set by round~2 all share one cause: \textbf{these models have already
saturated the task the benchmark was actually measuring}, which is correctness-preserving
rewriting rather than kernel optimisation. Any metric built on a saturated task piles up against
one wall or the other, and we tried three --- a headroom score, a pass-rate score, and a
456-task generated bank --- before recognising that.

\textbf{The substrate problem was therefore never a metric problem.} The remedy is not a better
scoring rule but a harder task, and the harder task only became measurable once the grader could
verify one. We regard this as the central practical lesson of the work: when a population sits
against a wall under every metric you try, the task is too easy, and no amount of re-normalising
will manufacture the variance that is missing.

\paragraph{Reachability: the dynamic-range precondition, made quantitative.}
``Check the metric has range'' is advice until it is a number, so we propose one.
\textbf{Reachability} is the fraction of bank tasks on which the \emph{frozen base} already
produces a submission clearing the scoring anchor. It is cheap --- it falls out of the same
calibration pass used to admit tasks --- and it is computed before any experiment runs.

Measured on the two banks we calibrated against an identical H100 anchor:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Bank} & \textbf{Tasks} & \textbf{Admitted} & \textbf{Reachability} \\
\midrule
Hand-curated (29-task) & 29 & 23 & 1 / 29 \quad(3.4\%) \\
Generated DSL (456-task) & 456 & 180 & 57 / 456 \quad(12.5\%) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Reachability = fraction of tasks where the frozen base clears the scoring anchor
($1.5\times$ over eager). Both banks are bimodal --- parity, or well past the anchor --- but
they differ four-fold in whether the speed dimension is reachable at all.}
\label{tab:reach}
\end{table}

A reachability of 3.4\% means that on 28 of 29 tasks, \emph{every} success the base produced
landed at parity, so the score had one attainable non-zero value and the contrast between two
arms was pinned before a single round ran. We would now decline to run a speed-scored
compounding study on such a bank, and we report the statistic for any bank we publish.

Note what this does \emph{not} say. The measurement above is against eager PyTorch at a fixed
$1.5\times$ anchor, which is what the default scorer uses; the roofline ceiling does not enter
it. It therefore licenses no claim about \texttt{torch.compile}, and a bank of \emph{generated}
references may be easy to beat for reasons that do not transfer --- a naive reference is not a
strong baseline. Reachability is a necessary condition for a usable substrate, not a sufficient
one.

\paragraph{Applying the precondition to our own headline.}
It would be evasive to propose reachability and not report it for the bank behind our own
A100 null. Per-task frozen-base scores were not retained for those runs --- an
artifact-retention failure we record as such --- so task-level reachability cannot be
reconstructed. What was retained is $C_0$, the frozen-base mean on each run's held split:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Scale} & \textbf{Runs} & \textbf{Median $C_0$} & \textbf{Runs with $C_0 > 0.51$} \\
\midrule
0.5B & 12 & 0.100 & 0\% \\
1.5B & 20 & 0.100 & 0\% \\
3B   & 19 & 0.381 & 0\% \\
7B   & 5  & 0.700 & 60\% \\
14B  & 1  & 0.599 & 100\% \\
\midrule
All  & 57 & 0.200 & 7\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent This matters because it shows the A100 substrate does \emph{not} carry the H100
failure signature. There, scores piled up at exactly $0.50$ --- correct-at-parity, an absorbing
state. Here the median is $0.20$, well \emph{below} parity, rising monotonically with scale.
Most held tasks were not solved at all rather than solved-at-parity, so the measurement had room
to move in the direction that mattered and the saturation critique does not transfer.

We state the cost of that defence in the same breath. A frozen base at $C_0 = 0.20$, with only
4 of 57 runs above parity, means the \emph{speed} dimension was barely exercised on A100 either:
most of the available signal was correctness, not optimisation. The A100 null is therefore best
read as a null about \emph{acquiring correctness through self-training}, which is a narrower
claim than the one the original framing implied --- and narrower than the title promised.

The general point is uncomfortable and worth stating plainly: \textbf{we could apply our own
proposed precondition to our own headline only partially, and only because a coarser statistic
happened to survive.} A precondition is worth little if the artifacts needed to evaluate it are
not retained, so we now record per-task frozen-base scores and stamp every artifact with the
environment that produced it.

\paragraph{What we propose as standard practice.}
We ship these as executable gates rather than advice, and we recommend them for any
self-improvement benchmark reporting a null:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{Known-good input, known-good output}, asserted before every batch and run
\emph{on the same allocation shape as the experiment} --- one of our defects lived entirely in
the difference between a 1-GPU and a 3-GPU allocation.
\item \textbf{A dynamic-range precondition}, reported as \emph{reachability}
(Table~\ref{tab:reach}): the fraction of tasks on which the frozen base already clears the
scoring anchor. Publish it beside every bank, and never interpret a contrast computed on a
bank where it is near zero.
\item \textbf{A positive control on the causal path}: an intervention known to move the primary
metric (for us, injecting verified solutions on tasks the student failed). A null is
interpretable only once the harness has been shown to register a positive.
\item \textbf{Mechanism denominators reported alongside every rate}, so undefined is
distinguishable from zero.
\item \textbf{Automated cross-artifact consistency checking}: every number in the manuscript
re-derived from the data files at build time. Ours catches stale figures and claim strings that
contradict the data they cite --- both of which occurred.
\end{enumerate}

We emphasise that this is not a catalogue of unusual carelessness. Each defect was a single
line, each was locally reasonable, and each was caught only because an assertion was looking
for it. The generalisable claim is structural: \textbf{in a benchmark whose headline is a
difference between two arms of one pipeline, the cheapest result to produce accidentally is a
null}, and the field currently has no convention requiring authors to rule that out.
